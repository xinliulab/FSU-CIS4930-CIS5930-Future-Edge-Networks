Course Objectives

  • Fundamental Goal: Applying AI/ML to 5G and Beyond. This course is designed to explore how machine learning, particularly deep learning, can be applied in modern wireless networks, especially in the context of 5G and beyond.
  • Core Focus: 5G/ORAN and Hybrid Routing Systems. We'll focus primarily on the 5G network architecture, especially on the Open Radio Access Network (ORAN). One of the central themes will be how to intelligently route models (i.e., model routing) in edge environments, selecting and placing ML models dynamically across the network. This intersects with both model routing and network routing, so students will learn how to design such hybrid systems from the ground up.
  • Key Topics and Emphasis on Edge-Side Challenges. The course will also touch on topics such as resource allocation, OFDM, and other essential concepts in edge wireless systems. While we'll briefly cover elements of the 5G core network (e.g., slicing strategies), the emphasis will be on edge-side challenges.

Prerequisites

    While no prior experience with wireless systems is required, students should be comfortable with programming (MatLab and Python will be used frequently) and have a basic understanding of machine learning, including some hands-on experience with deep learning techniques.

(W)		 Paper Practicum 2 (XG Architecture): Operating the Base Station — Efficiency, AI, Adversary
(Three student presentations, 15 min each + 5 min Q&A)
  1. RENC: Towards Energy Efficient 5G vRAN Servers (USENIX NSDI 2025) [Video][PDF]
    Why this paper: The RAN becomes a real-time computing system. A virtualized base station runs on ordinary CPU servers — but unlike a normal cloud workload, its baseband processing has sub-millisecond deadlines. RENC asks a question you can state in one line: can we slow the CPU down to save power without ever missing a deadline? Answer: up to 45% less CPU power, by coupling MAC-layer rate limiting to CPU frequency control.
  2. SAGE: A Real-Time AI System for Reducing Latency in NextG Cellular Networks (ACM SIGCOMM 2026) [Video][Slides][Author page]
    Why this paper: The RAN becomes AI-controlled. Today a phone must ask permission before it may transmit, and that round trip is the latency. SAGE predicts each user's uplink demand and grants resources before the request arrives — AI inside the millisecond control loop, not beside it.
  3. SNI5GECT: A Practical Approach to Inject aNRchy into 5G NR (USENIX Security 2025) [Video][Slides]
    Why this paper: 5G becomes a security attack surface. Sniff live 5G NR, decode it, track the protocol state, then inject your own frames at a real phone — no rogue base station required.

(M)		 Paper Practicum 3 (Wi-Fi): Why Your Video Call Stutters on Wi-Fi
(Three student presentations, 15 min each + 5 min Q&A)
  1. Zhuge: Achieving Consistent Low Latency for Wireless Real-Time Communications with the Shortest Control Loop (ACM SIGCOMM 2022) [Video][PDF][Code]
    Why this paper: your video call stutters even when bandwidth is plenty — the congestion feedback arrives too late. Zhuge shortens the control loop right at the Wi-Fi AP, the last hop you use every day.
  2. AFR: Enabling High Quality Real-Time Communications with Adaptive Frame-Rate (USENIX NSDI 2023) [Video][Slides][PDF][Code]
    Why this paper: when the network cannot keep up, the smartest move is to send fewer frames. Deployed in production cloud gaming for over a year.
  3. ACE: Sending Burstiness Control for High-Quality Real-time Communication (ACM SIGCOMM 2025) [Video][Slides][PDF]
    Why this paper: the video encoder spits out bursts, the network wants smooth traffic — the mismatch quietly adds tail latency at the sender. Winner of the IRTF Applied Networking Research Prize; the video and slides are the author's ANRP talk at IETF 125 (ACE starts partway into the session).

11 9/30
(W)		 Paper Practicum 4 (Wi-Fi): Inside 802.11 — Which Knobs Can We Still Redesign?
(Three student presentations, 15 min each + 5 min Q&A)
  1. Law: Towards Consistent Low Latency in 802.11 Home Networks (USENIX NSDI 2026) [Video][PDF]
    Why this paper: the queues, rate adaptation, and retries we met in the history lecture are exactly what cause latency spikes — so Law redesigns the link layer to bound them. It also exploits something only a video application can offer the link layer: a tolerance for losing a little.
  2. BLADE: Adaptive Wi-Fi Contention Control for Next-Generation Real-Time Communication (USENIX NSDI 2026) [Video][PDF]
    Why this paper: we spent the history lecture on CSMA/CA — sense, wait, back off a random amount, transmit. That random amount comes from a contention window the standard essentially fixes. BLADE asks why, and adapts every transmitter's window to the contention it actually sees: over 5× lower tail latency under heavy contention and 90% fewer cloud-gaming stalls, on commercial APs.
  3. SMUFF: Towards Line Rate Wi-Fi Direct Transport with Orchestrated On-device Buffer Management (USENIX NSDI 2024) [Video][PDF]
    Why this paper: two phones can already talk over Wi-Fi Direct at nearly a gigabit — so why does a file transfer never reach it? The bottleneck is not the PHY but the buffers stacked along the path inside the device: application, transport, socket, kernel, driver. Over one hop the sender can see every one of those buffers, so it can manage occupancy directly instead of guessing at congestion the way TCP must. Result: 94.7% of practical line rate.

(W)		 Paper Practicum 5 (ISAC): From Reference Signals to Vital Signs
(Three student presentations, 15 min each + 5 min Q&A)
  1. CARTS: Cooperative and Adaptive Resource Triggering and Stitching for 5G ISAC (ACM SenSys 2026) [PDF][arXiv]
    Why this paper: real 5G NR ISAC, with no new waveform at all. The uplink already carries two reference signals the standard put there for demodulation and channel sounding — DMRS and SRS. CARTS stitches those two asynchronous CSI streams together and decides when to trigger SRS, so the same transmission serves communication and localization at once: 85 cm tracking while supporting twice the users.
  2. CommRad: Context-Aware Sensing-Driven Millimeter-Wave Networks (ACM SenSys 2024) [Video][PDF][arXiv]
    Why this paper: the easiest entry point of the three, and the one with a talk video. A mmWave beam is narrow, so a walking user or a passing body breaks the link. CommRad puts a radar next to the base station and lets the two help each other — the radio tells the radar where to look, the radar keeps the beam on the user: 2.5× median and 8× 20th-percentile throughput.
  3. 2FiA: Towards WiFi Sensing-Based Authentication with Unique Biometrics (IEEE S&P 2026) [Artifact][Award]
    Why this paper: the far end of the idea. The CSI we met in the Wi-Fi lectures — the calibration data the chip already exposes — carries your breathing, and underneath it your heartbeat. 2FiA turns those two into an authentication factor, using the heartbeat precisely because it is the harder one to imitate. Winner of a Distinguished Artifact Award at IEEE S&P 2026 — and the authors include your instructor and your TA, so this is the one session where you can interrogate the authors directly. Paper PDF posted on Canvas.

(M)		 Paper Practicum 6 (Semantic Communication I): The Representation Shrinks
(Three student presentations, 15 min each + 5 min Q&A)
  1. Empilo: Realizing Immersive Mobile 3D Video Conferencing through Parameterized Communication (ACM MobiSys 2025) [Video]
    Why this paper: the representation at its smallest in this session: a compact set of face and scene parameters. The sender ships parameters only, and a neural renderer on the receiver generates the 3D scene from them.

Rather than the captured 3D representation, MagicStream extracts compact semantics of the user — appearance and motion — and reconstructs them at the far end. The demo video is the authors' side-by-side comparison against MetaStream, so watch it before reading either paper.

    Why this paper: the baseline this whole arc departs from, so read it last and look backwards. To place someone in your room in 3D, capture the full volumetric content and ship it — multi-camera calibration, edge-assisted segmentation, redundant-point removal and foveated rendering, all spent on reaching 30 fps over Wi-Fi. No talk video: it appears in the MagicStream comparison above.

(Three student presentations, 15 min each + 5 min Q&A)
  1. KDC: From Bits to Tokens: Knowledge-Driven Generative Communication of Multimodal Data (USENIX NSDI 2026) [Video][PDF]
    Why this paper: the representation stops being pixels or parameters and becomes tokens. A multimodal foundation model sits on both ends as shared knowledge, so the transmitter abstracts the source into tokenized embeddings and sends what the task actually needs rather than what the receiver could already infer.
  2. NIER: Practical Neural-enhanced Low-bitrate Video Conferencing (ACM SIGCOMM 2025) [Video][PDF]
    Why this paper: the same idea made to actually work in a video call at 10–100 Kbps: one reference image plus sparse keypoints per frame, plus the packet-loss handling a real conversation needs. The talk is inside the SIGCOMM'25 Shorts Session recording — the link starts it at 35:17.

    Why this paper: the receiver is no longer a person. When a multimodal LLM in the cloud is the one watching your video, the objective stops being visual quality and becomes the model's answer accuracy — so Artic caps bitrate where accuracy saturates and spends the remainder on the regions that determine the response.

Grading Policy

  • Assignments: 45%
    There will be several homework assignments (written and coding-based) spaced out over the course of the semester. All assignments are equally weighted. Submission instructions will be posted on GitHub Classroom and/or Canvas; most project submissions use GitHub.
  • Paper Practicum (Reading & Presentation): 15%
    Each student will complete one in-class paper practicum on top-tier conference papers related to Future Edge Networks.
    1. Format: Individual presentation only (one person per practicum). The same format applies to both undergraduate and graduate students: 15 minutes presentation + 5 minutes Q&A.
    2. Scheduling: Three papers are presented per class meeting. The practicum block runs for roughly seven weeks after the introductory lectures conclude.
    3. Paper selection: Papers will be assigned by the instructor. Students will first submit their preferred presentation dates (and optionally topics of interest). The instructor will then assign a paper to each student based on the course schedule and topic sequence.
    Important policy for paper practicum:
    1. In-person presentation only. No make-up or deferred practicums will be approved.
    2. Presentation time slots will be assigned in random order. Plan ahead when submitting your availability.
  • Final Project Report & Presentation: 40%
    1. Final presentation (15%) will take place at the end of the semester (dates subject to change). Each person will have 15 minutes for presentation and 5 minutes for Q&A.
    2. Demo (25%) You may choose whether or not to share the demo code. However, you must present a working demo during your project presentation.
    Important policy for final presentation:
    1. In-person presentation only. No make-up or deferred presentations will be approved.
    2. Multiple time slots will be assigned for each class in random order among groups. Plan ahead when submitting your availability.
  • Extra Bonus.
    Students are encouraged to prepare submissions to arXiv or major AI/ML/DM conferences based on their projects. Please make an appointment with the instructor prior to any submission plan for a comprehensive evaluation of the research topic. Each submission under the instructor's recognition will gain 2 points on the final grade.
