  \vspace{0.9em}
  {\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}r@{\hspace{0.6em}}l@{}}

    \textbf{BSC} & Base Station Controller\\
    \textbf{MSC} & Mobile Switching Center\\

  \vspace{0.6em}

   sit at every tower. Deciding to hand a call from one cell to the next has to
   see several towers at once. Those two jobs stopped fitting in one box.\par}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \takeaway{Digital identities made roaming scalable.}
\end{frame}

% Teaching note (2 min): before showing the answer, ask the class to look back at
% the GPRS diagram and find the two boxes missing from the EPC diagram.
%
% ACCURACY NOTE, do not flatten this again: in Release 8 EPC only the MME became a
% pure control node. S-GW and P-GW kept BOTH planes -- GTP-C signaling, IP address
% allocation, policy enforcement, charging. The clean control/user cut of the
% gateways is Release 14 CUPS (2017), and the "traffic grows faster than decisions"
% argument below is CUPS's own stated rationale, not Release 8's.
% https://www.3gpp.org/news-events/3gpp-news/cups
\begin{frame}{Wait --- where did SGSN and GGSN go?}
  \gonebox
    {SGSN tracked where you were and kept your data session alive.
     GGSN was the door out to the Internet.

     \vspace{0.4em}
     Each box did both jobs at once: it made decisions \textbf{and} it
     carried your traffic.}
    {Traffic grew far faster than decisions did.

     \vspace{0.4em}
     Ten times the video does not mean ten times the number of
     attach requests. But because one box did both, carrying more
     data meant buying more decision-making you did not need.}
    {4G \textbf{started} the cut. MME decides and never touches your packets.

     \vspace{0.4em}
     The gateways moved packets --- but kept deciding too. Finishing the
     split took until 2017.}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\small The clean idea arrived in one release. The clean \textbf{implementation}
   took nine more years.\par}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \takeaway{The box did not fail. It was doing two jobs with one growth rate.}
\end{frame}

% Teaching note (2 min): the MSC has been on every diagram since 2G. Point at the
% 4G diagram and let the class notice it is gone before answering.
%
% ACCURACY NOTE: an earlier draft said nothing replaced it "at first". Wrong, and it
% skipped the acronym students will actually meet in a fallback log. What replaced
% the MSC at first was the MSC, reached by falling back: CSFB (3GPP TS 23.272).
% SMS also kept reaching a legacy MSC/VLR over the SGs interface from the MME, and
% SRVCC handed VoLTE calls back to the CS domain when LTE coverage dropped. The CS
% core stayed in production for years. "Demoted, not replaced" is the honest beat.
\begin{frame}{And the MSC? It had been there since 2G.}
  \gonebox
    {The MSC was a telephone switch that happened to serve mobiles.

     \vspace{0.4em}
     It set up a circuit for each call and held that circuit open
     until somebody hung up.}
    {LTE's own bearers carried IP and nothing else.

     \vspace{0.4em}
     A circuit switch has no place in a packet network. Keeping one
     meant running a second network beside the first, with its own
     equipment, staff, and bill.}
    {At first, the MSC itself.

     \vspace{0.4em}
     An LTE phone that wanted to call \textbf{dropped back} to 2G or 3G to
     do it. Texts still reached a legacy switch.

     \vspace{0.4em}
     It was not replaced. It was demoted.}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \glossline{CSFB = Circuit-Switched Fallback, the standardized way an LTE phone
    borrowed an older network to make a call}

  \ask{So who finally carries your voice call on an all-IP network?}
\end{frame}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \qa{Why was ``all-IP'' a bigger change than making the radio faster?}
     {Speed is one number. All-IP changed the structure: one packet transport
      carries everything, and voice becomes an application on top of it instead
      of its own circuit-switched network.}
\end{frame}
