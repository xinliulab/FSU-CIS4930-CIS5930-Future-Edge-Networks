One antenna sprays energy everywhere

Four antennas: the copies add or cancel

0°
AP adds 0° per antenna → copies arrive at 0°, −90°, −180°, −270° → they cancel. Power at the client: 0.00×.
Power = length². Same total power split four ways: each antenna gets ¼ of the power, so its copy has length ½. In step: 4 × ½ = 2 → power 2² = 4×. Around a square: 0 — a null (a direction where the copies cancel). The 4× is paid for by less power in other directions.
180 s. Left: the AP. Each dial is the phase the AP gives that antenna; the grey row underneath is what the trip adds (antenna 1 is nearest, each next one a quarter turn later). Right: the client's view. The four copies are drawn head to tail; the thick arrow is what the client actually receives; the dashed circle is 1×, one antenna at full power. Point at the text line: power is length squared, so a quarter of the power is a copy of length one half. Default: the same phase on every antenna. The copies arrive a quarter turn apart and walk around a square back to zero: a null exactly on our client. Not bad luck: four quarter turns always close a square. Now drag the slider slowly to +90 (or press "beamform"): the square opens into a straight line, length 2, power 4×. Energy is not created: other directions get less (next slide shows where). Choose the phases: a beam

+90°
+90° per antenna → the beam points at 30°. Power at the client: 4.00× (6.0 dB).
+90° per antenna undoes the quarter turn the trip adds: all four copies arrive in step at 30°
→ 4× the power (6 dB) from the same total transmit power. That is beamforming.

What 4× (6 dB) buys

Faster. 6 dB more signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) ≈ one step up Class 8's QAM ladder (quadrature amplitude modulation): 64-QAM → 256-QAM, 6 → 8 bits per symbol.
Farther. In free space power falls as 1/d², so 4× the power reaches 2× the distance. Indoors, walls make it less.
Two clients at once. Two beams in one transmission, each with a null on the other client: MU-MIMO (multi-user multiple-input multiple-output, Class 8).
Client A at 30°, client B at −40°: each beam is aimed at its client and has a null on the other.
All of it needs the right phase for every antenna. Who knows those phases?
symbol = one tick of the radio, carrying a few bits · QAM = the grid of points a symbol can land on (Class 8's constellation): 64 points carry 6 bits, 256 carry 8
120 s. The payoff in numbers. Rate: Class 8's constellation slide — every step up the QAM ladder needs more SNR; going from 64 to 256-QAM (2 more bits per symbol) costs about 6 dB, exactly what four antennas bought. Range: power falls as 1/d² in free space, so 4× power is 2× distance; indoors less. MU-MIMO: the AP shapes two beams at once; each puts a null on the OTHER client so they do not hear each other's data (the right panel is computed: zero-forcing weights for clients at 30° and −40°). Same air both ways, different radios: so the AP asks

Question 2 of 6: why must the AP ask the client?
Why now: the right phases come from h — and Class 12's AP measured h itself. Next (Question 3): how it asks.
  • The air between antenna n and the client is the same both ways (reciprocity). But each AP antenna sends through one circuit and listens through another, each adding its own unknown phase.
  • So uplink CSI is not what the AP's beam will meet unless the AP keeps every circuit calibrated to within a few degrees — fiddly. 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4) made that “implicit” route optional, and few devices implemented it interoperably; 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5) standardised only the simpler explicit way: the client measures and reports.
150 s. Head off the obvious objection first: last week the AP measured h from the phone's packets, so why ask? Because of the picture. The radio wave between antenna n and the client sees the same air both ways — that is reciprocity. But inside the AP each antenna has two different circuits: one for transmitting, one for receiving, each adding its own phase (t_n and r_n). What the AP hears on the uplink is h_n times r_n; what its beam meets on the downlink is h_n times t_n. Those differ per antenna, are unknown, and drift with temperature and gain settings, so phases computed from the uplink would point the beam the wrong way unless the AP keeps every chain calibrated — to within a few degrees, all the time. That is fiddly, which is the honest reason implicit beamforming faded: 802.11n (2009, Wi-Fi 4) made calibration and the implicit route optional alongside several explicit ones, and few devices implemented them interoperably. 802.11ac standardised only the explicit route: the client measures a test packet and sends back compressed feedback. Keep t_n in mind: it comes back on slide 24 to bias the sniffer. (And the client's own circuits? Our client has one antenna, so its receive-circuit phase multiplies all four h_n alike — a common phase, which cancels like any other.) And h changes whenever someone walks by, so sounding happens over and over.

Step 1: a heads-up (NDPA)

Question 3 of 6: how does the AP ask? (“sounding”)
Why now: the client must measure h on one agreed packet. Next (Question 4): what it sends back.
Clients ignore frames not addressed to them, and the measurement must happen on one agreed packet. So the AP first says: “a measuring packet follows right after this one — you and you: measure it and report.”
That heads-up is the NDPA (Null Data Packet Announcement), a short control frame. No measuring happens here.
Sensing hook: a sniffer that reads the NDPA learns whose report comes next — the AIDs name the clients.
Control frame = a short frame that coordinates who uses the air (management frames, like the report later, do housekeeping) · STA = station (a client) · AID = Association ID, a client's number on its AP · SU / MU = single- / multi-user · data stream = one independent flow of symbols; a 1-antenna client can receive one · FCS = frame check sequence (a checksum)
120 s. Problem first: a client normally drops frames that are not for it, and a measurement is only useful if client and AP agree on WHICH packet was measured. So step one is a warning. The grey fields are routine (frame type, how long the air is reserved, receiver and transmitter address, a checksum) — do not dwell on them. The two that matter: the sounding dialog token — a ticket number the client copies into its report so the AP can match answer to sounding — and one STA Info per client: its AID (the small number it was given when it associated), single-user or multi-user feedback, and how many columns of the report to send back (the field is Nc; one per data stream; one for our 1-antenna client — the report itself comes in Question 4). Then the name: Null Data Packet Announcement; 802.11ac calls it the VHT NDP Announcement. Read the sensing hook: a sniffer learns from the AIDs whose report is coming.

Step 2: a packet that is all ruler (NDP)

To measure h for all 4 AP antennas on every subcarrier, the client needs symbols it already knows, sent from all 4 antennas — and no payload.
Sensing hook: the LTFs are where the CSI is born — every angle in today's lecture starts in these four boxes.
NDP = Null Data Packet: a preamble and nothing else — a 52 µs measuring stick.
Every LTF is sent from all four antennas at once. How can the client tell them apart? Next slide.
90 s. Top: Class 8's packet, preamble + header + payload. Bottom: the sounding packet, drawn to scale: preamble only. Wave at the grey boxes (short training fields detect the packet and set the gain; SIG fields are headers; L- fields are the legacy part old devices can read) and spend the time on the blue ones. Long training fields are symbols the client already knows, so comparing what arrived with what was sent gives h — exactly last week's CSI. Our AP has four antennas, so the NDP carries four VHT-LTFs (the standard says at least one per antenna sounded). Each LTF goes out of all four antennas at the same time — so how does the client separate h1 from h4? Leave that hanging for the next slide. 52 µs is about the time light needs to cross 16 km.

4 equations, 4 unknowns. The signs are chosen so each h falls out by adding and subtracting: h1 = (y1 − y2 + y3 + y4) / 4.
The ± pattern across the four LTFs is what tells the antennas apart.
Solve it on every subcarrier (234 at 80 MHz) → the client holds the whole CSI grid.
Sensing hook: these h's are exactly the CSI we would love to have. The client computes them — but sends out only a compressed version (Question 4).
90 s. The answer to the cliffhanger. Every antenna transmits in every LTF, but with a different sign each time (the table: rows are antennas, columns are LTFs; this is the 4×4 sign matrix 802.11n/ac use for four streams). Read the first column aloud: in LTF 1, antennas 1–3 send +, antenna 4 sends −, so the client receives h1 + h2 + h3 − h4. Four LTFs give four such equations. CS framing: a 4×4 linear system per subcarrier, and because the rows of the sign table are orthogonal, solving it is just adding and subtracting: h1 = (y1 − y2 + y3 + y4)/4 — check one term on the board if the room is with you (h2 cancels: +1 +1 −1 −1). Same for every subcarrier: that is the CSI grid of Question 4, and it never leaves the client's chip in raw form.

Step 3: the report — the whole exchange in under 0.5 ms

SIFS (Short Interframe Space) = a fixed 16 µs gap. Everyone else must wait longer, so nobody cuts in.
More clients: 802.11ac asks them one at a time (a Beamforming Report Poll each); 802.11ax triggers them all to answer at once.
Sensing hook: the dark box — the client's report — is the frame we will capture. h changes whenever someone moves, so the AP keeps re-sounding: each report is a fresh snapshot of the room (how often is the vendor's choice).
120 s. Press → four times (or open with ?all to see all of it). (1) NDPA from the AP, about 56 µs for one client at the 6 Mb/s base rate (less at higher rates). (2) A SIFS gap, then the NDP, 52 µs. (3) Another SIFS, then the CLIENT's report: drawn at 200 µs, really 100–300 µs depending on how many subcarriers, how many bits and the rate. (4) After one more SIFS the AP sends data, beamformed. Adding it up: 56 + 16 + 52 + 16 + (100 to 300) + 16 ≈ 0.26–0.46 ms. Why SIFS works: it is the shortest gap in Wi-Fi; Class 8's CSMA/CA makes every other device wait at least a DIFS (34 µs at 5 GHz) plus a random backoff, so nobody can squeeze in between. Several clients: 802.11ac follows the first report with a Beamforming Report Poll to each next client; 802.11ax (HE, High Efficiency) sends one BFRP trigger frame and the clients answer together, each on its own slice of subcarriers (OFDMA: OFDM with the subcarriers shared among users). Sense of scale: a 60 fps video frame lasts 16.7 ms. How often any of this happens is the vendor's call — watch for it in the demo.

The client now holds last week's CSI

Question 4 of 6: what does the client send back?
Why now: the NDP just gave the client h. Next: what it computes from h, and how it squeezes that into bits.
  • One complex h per AP antenna per subcarrier — exactly Class 12's CSI, measured at the client this time.
  • Send it all? 4 antennas × 234 subcarriers = 936 complex numbers; at 8 bits per real and per imaginary part (an assumption) ≈ 1.9 kB per sounding, per client. And the AP does not need the channel itself — it needs the phases to transmit with.
So the client computes what the AP will use, and sends only that.
120 s. The grid is the CSI the client just measured: one arrow per (AP antenna, subcarrier). Down a column the arrows turn a quarter turn per antenna — that is the 30° direction, last week's angle ruler. Along a row they twist steadily — that is the delay, last week's distance ruler. (Only 14 of the subcarriers drawn.) Ask: why not just send all of it? Two reasons: size — 936 complex numbers, 1.9 kB if each part took 8 bits (the 8 is our assumption, just for scale), every sounding, for every client — and need: the AP is not trying to understand the room, it wants to know what to transmit. Compress (1): the phases become φ

30°
θ = 30°: φ11 = 90°, φ21 = 180°, φ31 = 270° — each next antenna +90° (a quarter turn).
  • Turn all four arrows together until antenna 4's points right: antenna 4 is the zero (the reference; it sends no φ).
  • φk1 = angle of antenna k measured from antenna 4. At 30°: 90°, 180°, 270°, and antenna 4 is 0° = 360°.
  • Neighbours differ by 90°: Class 12's quarter-turn step. Here each next antenna leads by 90° instead of lagging, because v = conj(h) flips every angle.
For one stream, the gaps between neighbouring φ's are Class 12's phase steps, with the sign flipped.
The standard makes the last antenna the zero; slide 17's fingerprint starts from antenna 1. Only differences matter, so which antenna is zero is a free choice.

Compress (2): the lengths become ψ

Each cut is one angle ψ: cos ψ and sin ψ split a length into two parts whose squares add to 1. Count: 4 complex numbers = 8 real numbers, − 1 common phase, − 1 fixed length = 6 angles: φ11, φ21, φ31, ψ21, ψ31, ψ41.
Relax about ψ: in our example it carries no direction (equal shares; ψ41 = 30° is a coincidence, not θ).
The angle lives in φ. ψ matters only when reflections make the antennas unequal.
150 s. The φ's carried the phases; the ψ's carry how the power (length squared) is shared among the antennas. Section done: the BFM is v, written as six angles per subcarrier, sent in the clear.

Multipath: every measurement is a mix

hk = w1(k) · a(30°) + w2(k) · a(55°) + noise
Each snapshot hk = the CSI of one subcarrier of one packet: two fingerprints, mixed. The weights change from snapshot to snapshot (each path has its own delay and phase).
Simulated: direct path 30°, reflection 55° (70% as strong), 50 subcarriers × 20 packets = 1000 snapshots. The dB axis is relative to the curve's own peak.

120 s. Real rooms again. Each CSI snapshot h_k (one subcarrier of one packet) is a weighted sum of two steering vectors, one per path. The weights differ per subcarrier because the two paths have different delays (Class 12's subcarrier twist, a different twist per path) and per packet because people move by fractions of a wavelength. Run last week's sweep on this data (simulated, but realistic): one fat lobe whose peak, 34.5°, is neither the direct path nor the reflection. MUSIC's idea: dots on a tabletop

  • Every snapshot is a mix of a(30°) and a(55°), so all of them lie flat on one “table”: the signal subspace.
  • Straight up from the table there is nothing but noise: the noise subspace. Test each a(θ): flat on the table → a path.
The real data has more than 3 dimensions; the picture is a cartoon of it.
180 s. THE intuition frame of the section; go slowly. Each dot is one snapshot h_k. Because every snapshot is (something) × a(30°) + (something) × a(55°), all the dots lie on the flat plane spanned by those two arrows: a tabletop in a room. Nothing in the data ever points "up" off the table, except noise. Now the test: take a candidate steering vector, say a(0°), and ask how much of it sticks up out of the table (the red dashed drop). A lot → not a path. a(30°) lies exactly in the table → sticks up by zero → 1 / zero = a huge score. That is the whole algorithm. Ask the room: what if all the dots were the SAME mix? (They would sit on one line, not a table — slide 21 shows MUSIC failing then.) Honesty line: the real space is 4 complex dimensions, the table is 2-D, "up" is 2-D; the picture is the 3-D cartoon.

MUSIC in three steps

How much the snapshots spread along each of R's 4 eigenvectors (its eigenvalues): 2 big, 2 tiny.
  1. Average h hH over all snapshots → a 4×4 matrix R (the covariance): how the antennas vary together.
  2. Eigenvectors of R: the big-eigenvalue ones span the table — count them: the number of paths. The small ones span “up”; call them En.
  3. Scan θ:  P(θ) = 1 / ‖EnH a(θ)‖²
    = 1 / (how much a(θ) sticks up). Flat → a peak.
Why eigenvectors find the table: R stretches most along the directions the dots spread out along — so its big eigenvectors lie in the table. Noise spreads a tiny bit in every direction; along the table it is drowned by the signal, so the leftover directions contain only noise.
MUSIC = MUltiple SIgnal Classification (Schmidt, 1986). It needs at least one “up” direction left over,
so N antennas find at most N − 1 paths: 3 for our AP.
eigenvector = a direction the matrix only stretches, never turns; eigenvalue = how much it stretches · hH = flip every angle, turn the column into a row
180 s. The tabletop, in linear algebra. Step 1: average the outer products h h^H; R records how the four antennas vary together. Step 2: eigen-decompose R (one library call, numpy.linalg.eigh). Directions where the snapshots spread a lot (big eigenvalues) span the table; the left plot is computed from our simulated data: two big (two paths), two essentially zero (noise only). Step 3: for each candidate θ, project a(θ) onto the "up" vectors; the squared length of that projection is how much it sticks up; P(θ) is one over it. Name and citation last: MUSIC, Ralph Schmidt, 1986. Limit: the table must leave at least one "up" direction, so N antennas see at most N−1 paths. Our AP: 3.

The report is the CSI minus what angle doesn't need

Question 6 of 6: can the BFM give the same angles?
Why now: MUSIC needs snapshots that lie on the table. Next: run it on rebuilt BFM vectors and compare.
  • For our 1-antenna client the chip only flips and shrinks each h. Conjugate v back and you get h itself, shorter and turned by one common phase, so it still lies on MUSIC's table.
  • The chip drops one common phase and the length per subcarrier; angle never needed them. A common phase cancels: (ejαv)(ejαv)H = ejαe−jα v vH = v vH.
  • MUSIC still does its own decomposition across all the snapshots, exactly as on CSI.
Footnote, clients with several antennas: V's columns are eigenvectors of HHH for each subcarrier — there the chip does run a decomposition per subcarrier.
150 s. Two routes into the same MUSIC code. Top: last week's, CSI straight from a modified card. Bottom: today's. For our 1-antenna client the chip did nothing clever: it turned each subcarrier's h into a unit vector along conj(h) — rotate and shrink — wrote it as six angles and sent them. We rebuild v from the angles with the formula three slides back, conjugate it (v points the arrows backwards, h forwards), and feed it to MUSIC as if it were CSI. Two worries to dismiss: (1) the client threw away one common phase per subcarrier — v v^H is blind to a common phase, so MUSIC never sees it; (2) each v has length 1 instead of ‖h‖ — that only reweights the snapshots, the table is the same. MUSIC's own eigen-decomposition across snapshots is still our job. Only for multi-antenna clients does the chip run a per-subcarrier decomposition (the SVD) — the footnote.

What the BFM keeps — and what it erases

Cancels — good

  • Carrier phase and CFO
  • Timing offsets STO, SFO

Last week's hardware dirt: the same on all 4 antennas at a subcarrier, so it goes away with the common phase.

Lost — bad

  • Distance. Time of flight is also a phase common to all antennas at each subcarrier. Each subcarrier is reset separately, so Chronos's slope across subcarriers is wiped.
  • Absolute strength (only an average SNR per stream)

Still there: the AP's own phases

  • Each AP antenna's transmit phase tn (slide 7) rides inside every v, so the absolute angle is biased until calibrated. The same tn that forced the AP to ask now bias the sniffer.
  • Without owning the AP: calibrate with a device at a known spot, or track changes in angle.
  • Also: a few bits per angle; samples only when the AP sounds.
Anything common to all antennas at one subcarrier vanishes — last week's dirt, and the distance with it.
The angle lives in the differences between antennas, so it survives.
CFO = carrier frequency offset · STO = symbol timing offset · SFO = sampling frequency offset (Class 12's offset table) · Time of flight = the propagation delay, Chronos's distance ruler
120 s. One rule generates all three boxes: per subcarrier, the client normalized v (length 1, antenna 4 real). Anything that multiplies all four antennas by the same number at that subcarrier disappears. Green: CFO, the carrier phase offset, and the timing offsets (STO, SFO) were exactly that kind of dirt last week — they cancel for free. Red: the time of flight is ALSO a phase common to all antennas at each subcarrier; since every subcarrier is normalized on its own, Chronos's slope across subcarriers is wiped, so the distance ruler is gone; BFM gives angle, not range. The AP's owner could; a sniffer who does not own the AP has practical options — put a reference device at a known spot and measure the offsets, or ignore the absolute value and track changes in angle (someone walking across the room). Same t_n that made implicit beamforming fiddly. Plus quantization and grouping, and the AP, not you, decides how often a sample arrives.

The payoff: an angle from the air — no special card, no key

  • Any Wi-Fi card in monitor mode (it keeps every frame it hears) captures the report — from every 802.11ac/ax client its AP beamforms to.
  • Even on WPA2/WPA3: Wi-Fi's protection for management frames (802.11w) covers only a list of “robust” frame types; the beamforming report is not on the list.
  • Wireshark captures and labels the frame; the open-source Wi-BFI tool turns the angles back into V.

120 s. The payoff, not a recap. CSI needed a patched driver or firmware on a device you control (slide 1). The BFM is sent by every Wi-Fi 5/6 client its AP beamforms to, as a plain management frame. Wi-Fi does protect management frames (802.11w, "protected management frames") — but only a listed set of "robust" frame types, and the VHT/HE beamforming report (VHT = 802.11ac, HE = High Efficiency, 802.11ax's name for itself) is not on that list, so even on a WPA3 network it goes out in the clear. Any card in monitor mode captures it; Wireshark shows the frame and its fields, and Wi-BFI (Haque, Meneghello & Restuccia, WiNTECH 2023) exists precisely because no common tool turned the angles back into V. Pointers: BFMSense (Yi et al., NSDI 2024) derives the BFM–CSI relation and tracks breathing and walking; BeamSense (Haque, Zhang, Meneghello & Restuccia, Computer Networks 258, 2025; arXiv 2023) uses multi-user feedback for activity recognition. The same property that makes it convenient is the privacy problem we end on.

Take-homes

  1. 1Beamforming: pre-rotate each antenna so the copies arrive in step. Four antennas → 4× the power (6 dB) at the client.
  2. 2Uplink CSI isn't enough (different transmit and receive circuits), so the AP sounds: NDPA (heads-up) → NDP (ruler) → report.
  3. 3The report is the BFM: v = conj(h)/‖h‖ as 6 angles — φ = phase steps between antennas, ψ = how the length is split — a few bits each.
  4. 4MUSIC: varied snapshots lie on a “table”; the directions whose steering vector lies flat on it are the paths.
  5. 5BFM vectors lie on that table too: the same angles, from frames anyone can capture — but no distance.
