Where is the phone?

Every one of these needs a position — indoors,
where the one positioning system everybody knows does not reach.
Figure: Adib & Eid, MIT MAS.S60 Lecture 2, “Fundamentals of Wireless Sensing & Localization”
150 s. Start with the want, not the technique. Walk the photos: a museum map on your phone, VR, a delivery drone. All of them need "where am I" INDOORS. Ask who has used Google Maps inside a mall, and how well it went.

Outdoors: GPS. Indoors: nothing good

Every building already has Wi-Fi.
Can the access point itself work out where the phone is?
Figure: Xiong & Jamieson, ArrayTrack talk, NSDI 2013
120 s. GPS = meters, outdoors only; indoors it fades. Point at the red lines: known indoor options are either not accurate (Wi-Fi RSSI) or need new hardware (ultrasound) or cameras. Then the observation the lecture rides on.

Who does the locating?

Today: network-based. The AP hears the phone's packet anyway. What can it measure from it?
signal strength  →  phase  →  angle  →  distance  →  both
Figure: MIT MAS.S60 Lecture 2
150 s. Two ways to run localization. GPS: the DEVICE listens to anchors. Radar / Wi-Fi: the NETWORK listens to the device. Today is the second kind — the AP hears the phone's packet anyway. What can it measure from that packet? Announce the ladder: power → phase → angle → distance.

Attempt 1: louder means closer

Power ∝ 1/d2: read a power, get a distance.
One distance per AP; the circles meet at the phone.
Figures: MIT MAS.S60 Lecture 2
150 s. Left: power falls as 1/d², so a power reading is a distance. Right: three APs, three circles, one intersection — trilateration, exactly what GPS does with time. This is how every phone OS started.

Why RSSI stops at room level

Reflections add and cancel: power wiggles with position.
Workaround: record a fingerprint at every dot.
One number per packet, summed over every reflection: room-level.
For a desk-level answer we need a finer ruler.
Figures: MIT MAS.S60 Lecture 2
180 s. Two cons on the left curve — far away the curve is flat (a small power error is a large distance error), and reflections make power wiggle with position. Right: the industry workaround — walk the building and record a fingerprint at every orange dot. Works; room-level; must be redone when furniture moves. Verdict box, then move on.

The signal is a wave; the phase is a ruler

  • Amplitude decays — that was RSSI. Phase turns 360° every wavelength.
  • 5 GHz: λ = 6 cm, so 1° of phase ≈ 0.17 mm of path.
  • The card already computes h per subcarrier, per antenna, to decode (Class 8): that is CSI.
CSI = Channel State Information: the h values the receiver estimated from the packet preamble
Figure: MIT MAS.S60 Lecture 2
210 s. Trace the figure with a finger. Amplitude decays — that was RSSI. Underneath, the phase rotates one full turn per wavelength. At 5 GHz a wavelength is 6 cm, so one degree of phase is 0.17 mm of path. The complex number h is what the Wi-Fi card computes for every subcarrier and every antenna just to decode the packet (Class 8) — the industry calls the collection CSI.

… but the ruler has no numbers on it

Every red tick looks identical to the receiver. A 5 m path has turned ≈ 87 times; you see only the leftover fraction.
You cannot read one phase. You can read the difference between two:
two antennas → angle;    two frequencies → distance.
Figure: Vasisht, Kumar & Katabi, Chronos talk, NSDI 2016
180 s. The catch. The red ticks are every moment the phase reads the same value: one period apart, 0.4 ns, 12 cm. A phone 5 m away has turned 87 full times; the AP sees only the last fraction of a turn. So a single phase reading is worthless — and the whole rest of the lecture is two ways of reading a DIFFERENCE instead: two antennas (angle) or two frequencies (distance).

Two antennas hear the wave at two phases

Antenna 2 is λ2 sin θ farther, so it lags by π sin θ:
θ = arcsin( ∠x2 − ∠x1π )
Phone at 30°: a quarter turn per antenna.
Spacing is λ/2 so the step itself never wraps.
Figure: ArrayTrack, NSDI 2013 (as redrawn in Princeton COS463 Lab 5)
210 s. THE geometry frame; go slowly. Antenna 2 is half a wavelength to the right; a wave from angle θ travels an extra (λ/2) sin θ to reach it, i.e. lags by π sin θ. On the I/Q plane that is the green arc between x1 and x2. Invert: θ = arcsin(phase step / π). Running example: 30° → a quarter turn. Ask why λ/2: any wider and the step itself wraps.

More antennas: a spectrum of power vs. bearing

N antennas, N − 1 phase steps: sweep θ in software.
A peak in the spectrum is a bearing.
Two APs' bearings cross at the phone.
Figures: MIT MAS.S60 Lecture 2 (left); ArrayTrack talk, NSDI 2013 (right)
180 s. N antennas = N−1 steps; sweep a candidate angle in software and ask how well the measured steps fit — the fit versus angle is the AoA spectrum (right). A peak is a bearing; two APs' bearings cross at the client. Say "the beam is a for-loop run after reception".

The office fights back: multipath

Three lobes in the spectrum. Which one is the phone?
Figure: ArrayTrack talk, NSDI 2013
150 s. Reality. Photo: a real office. Diagram: the client's signal reaches the array directly (grey), off the wall (green), off furniture (blue) — and the spectrum honestly shows ALL of them. Worse: the direct path may be the weakest. Leave the question hanging for a beat.

ArrayTrack's trick: the direct path holds still

Move the client a few cm. Reflected bearings swing; the direct bearing stays. Keep the peaks that reappear within 5°.
Figures: ArrayTrack talk, NSDI 2013
180 s. ArrayTrack's answer is physical, not mathematical. Move the client a few centimetres: a reflection's bearing swings (the bounce point moves), the direct bearing barely changes. Take spectra from two nearby positions and keep only peaks that reappear within five degrees.

More APs, sharper dot: 23 cm

One AP gives a bearing, not a point. Six 8-antenna APs: 23 cm median.
Angle alone needs several APs around the room.
Your home has one.
Figures: ArrayTrack talk, NSDI 2013
150 s. The payoff and the price. One AP gives a bearing, not a point; six APs sharpen the heat-map to a dot, 23 cm median. The price is in the hardware: six APs, each with 8 antennas, around one room. Last line of the frame is the segue — your home has one AP.

Homes have ONE access point

One AP needs angle and distance. Distance = c × propagation delay, and the delay sits in the phase — wrapped.
Figures: Chronos talk, NSDI 2016
120 s. The constraint that motivates everything: homes and shops have ONE access point. One AP can give a bearing; to pin a point it also needs the distance. And distance = speed of light × propagation delay, which lives in the phase — wrapped, as we saw.

Many frequencies agree on only one delay

Each frequency wraps at its own period. Only the true delay is consistent with all of them.
Same sentence as last section: a difference across a small step beats wrapping — now the step is in frequency, not space.
Figure: Chronos talk, NSDI 2016
210 s. THE intuition frame of the section. One frequency: the ticks repeat every period, so the delay is ambiguous. Add a second frequency: it repeats at a different period. Only at the TRUE delay do the ticks of every frequency line up (the green check). Mathematicians call it the Chinese remainder theorem; the class can call it "find the time when all four clocks agree".

OFDM hands you the frequencies for free

Phase vs. subcarrier is a straight line; its slope is the delay. 5 m path: ≈ 120° of fall across one 20 MHz packet.
You have known this since Class 8: every packet is 52+ equally spaced tones, each reporting its own phase.
Figure: Chronos talk, NSDI 2016
180 s. Where do many frequencies come from, measured at the same instant? OFDM — Class 8. A Wi-Fi packet is 52+ subcarriers Δ apart, each with its own phase. Plot phase against subcarrier: a straight line whose slope is the delay. For a 5 m path the line falls ~120° across a 20 MHz packet.

Multipath: the shortest delay is the distance

IFFT across subcarriers: one spike per path.
A reflection always travels farther. 5.2 ns × c = 1.6 m to the phone.
Figures: Chronos talk, NSDI 2016 (left); Yang et al., Tsinghua “Hands-on Wireless Sensing” tutorial (right)
180 s. Multipath, resolved honestly. Each path has its own delay; transform the phase-vs-frequency line back to time (IFFT across subcarriers) and you get the channel impulse response — one spike per path. Reflections travel farther by definition, so the SHORTEST delay is the distance to the phone.

The catch: the receiver's own delay

  • Detection delay is larger than propagation delay and changes every packet.
  • Chronos: the phase at the carrier is immune — read it off the OFDM line; a packet/ACK pair cancels clock offsets.
  • Resolution is bandwidth: 20 MHz separates paths only ~15 m apart, so Chronos hops across all Wi-Fi bands.
Figure: Chronos talk, NSDI 2016
210 s. The honesty frame. The AP cannot see the propagation delay alone: it sees propagation + its own packet-DETECTION delay, which is larger and differs on every packet. Chronos's two moves: the phase at the carrier frequency is immune to detection delay (read it off the OFDM line), and a packet/ACK exchange cancels the unknown clock offset. Also the resolution limit: a 20 MHz packet separates delays only ~15 m apart, so Chronos hops across every Wi-Fi band.

What a single access point achieves

Distance: 14 cm (LOS), 21 cm (NLOS)
Right room 94% of the time
Drone follows at 1.4 m, 4 cm error
One off-the-shelf Wi-Fi card. No infrastructure beyond the AP.
Figures: Chronos talk, NSDI 2016
120 s. What one stock AP buys you: 14 cm distance error with line of sight, the right room 94% of the time, a drone that keeps 1.4 m from its user to within 4 cm. Say the hardware out loud: an Intel 5300 Wi-Fi card.

Angle and delay from the same packet

  • 3 antennas × 30 subcarriers = 90 sensors in one packet's CSI.
  • Every path becomes an (angle, delay) pair; fit them jointly.
  • Direct path = the earliest pair with a stable bearing. Then cross the bearings.
Figure 1: Kotaru, Joshi, Bharadia & Katti, SpotFi, SIGCOMM 2015
210 s. Walk the four panels left to right. Collect CSI at every AP. Each path shows up as a pair (angle, delay). The direct path is the one with the smallest delay AND a stable bearing (ArrayTrack's trick, reused). Intersect the direct bearings. The trick that makes it work on THREE antennas: treat every (antenna, subcarrier) cell as a sensor — 90 sensors per packet, not 3.

A stock 3-antenna AP: 40 cm median

Same office, same 3-antenna hardware: SpotFi 0.4 m median vs. 1.8 m for angle-only. Without line of sight: 1.6 m.
“Where is the phone?” — answered to 40 cm
by access points that were already on the wall.
Figure 7: SpotFi, SIGCOMM 2015
120 s. Same office, same three-antenna hardware: SpotFi 0.4 m median, ArrayTrack's algorithm 1.8 m. In the no-line-of-sight corridor SpotFi degrades to 1.6 m — say that too. Close the loop: the question on slide one is answered with stock access points and nothing else.
